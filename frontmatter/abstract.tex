\epigraph{Civilization advances by extending the number of important operations which we can perform without thinking about them}{Alfred North Whitehead, An Introduction to Mathematics}

\lettrine{A}{production-grade} optimizing compiler is a large program.
In LLVM, the peephole rewriter alone is larger than the entire vectorizer!
This thesis asks how such a compiler can be verified.
There is no hope for one person to verify all of it, so I chose to focus on the peephole rewriter,
whose unit of work is a single, local transformation on a finite window of instructions.
Each transformation is small enough to prove correct,
but the rewriter as a whole accumulates thousands of such proof obligations.

Can we prove them by hand? In principle, yes; in practice it is deathly tedious and error-prone,
as I discovered when I set out to verify an SSA-based peephole rewriter for LLVM's bitvector optimizations.
I first formalized a core calculus for SSA-based compiler intermediate representations,
generic over the user's IR, and proved a generic peephole rewriter correct,
with case studies ranging from fully homomorphic encryption to LLVM's bitvector rewrites.
It is the last of these where the real difficulty began.
The structural half of manipulating SSA came for free; the arithmetic half did not.
The obligations were subtle, and proving them by hand was slow enough that
the thousands of rewrites a production compiler relies on were plainly out of reach.
It is this tedium that drives the demand for push-button automation.

What style of decision procedure should we bet on?
I bet on bitblasting, almost by accident, since I'd been helping to implement \bvdecide{},
Lean's fixed-width bitblaster, which carries a bitvector goal all the way down to a SAT instance, solves it,
and has the resulting certificate rechecked by Lean's kernel.
This was fortuitous, as the infrastructre was designed to mirror Bitwuzla, a state of the art fixed width bitvector, floating point, and array solver.
Only later did I appreciate that our choices also power one of today's fastest SMT solvers.
I learnt a lot by studying Bitwuzla in the process, and this infrastructure in Lean is the bedrock of the rest of the thesis.
Furthermore, in the process of studying Bitwuzla, I was introduced to the circle of ideas
around monotone circuit complexity, circuit lower bounds, interpolation, herbrand models,
and various aspects of model theory, proof theory, and proof complexity.
This was very enriching, and I loved gaining a deeper sense of the boundary of SAT solving,
NP-completeness, and the role of bitblasting in the landscape of SMT solving.

However, the proof obligations in a compiler hands us are not bitblastable as they stand.
A rewrite has to be correct at \emph{every} register width, whereas a bitblaster wants one fixed width,
and the standard technique (for example, as used by the Alive peephole rewrite verifier) is to enumerate the widths and bitblast each one separately.
We show that this is unnecessary. Predicates that must hold at all widths reduce to reachability in a finite automaton,
which we decide by $k$-induction and language emptiness.
Next, by a neat encoding trick (that encodes widths as bitmasks),
any formula over $n$ symbolic widths reduces to an equisatisfiable single-width one.
This trades an exponential number of solver queries for a \emph{single} query,
and yields a new sound and complete fragment of the multi-width theory.
These round out my developments in bitvectors,
where I show that bitblasting as an approach that's effective for parametric bitvector theory.

Floating point was the next, complex, missing piece in order to have a fully verified Bitwuzla.
I studied the venerable SymFPU, which, in a sequence of papers, decisively proved that bitblasting
outcompetes other approaches to floating point SMT solving.
Toward having a mechanized floating point solver, We mechanized the SMT-LIB \qffp{} theory and build verified bitblasting circuits for almost all of its operations,
and proved correctness at every combination of exponent and mantissa width.
At the end of this epic journey, I learnt of a very interesting, alternative approach to floating-point,
which the practioners seem to care about: treat floating point as if they were real numbers, and decide a fragment of arithmetic that had all the field operations,
along with exponentiation and square root. I adapt algorithms introduced by the Triton team, 
into a new solver design that attempts to perform bounded verification of this logic by means of bitblasting,
which I sketch as an alternative to the IEEE-semantics based approach.

Did the bet on automation pay off? I think so.
Our width-generic solvers prove more all-width problems than the unverified state of the art,
and our floating-point bitblaster, while slower than Bitwuzla,
offers end-to-end guarantees down to Lean's kernel in exchange.
What I learnt is a flavour of the bitter lesson: the only thing that scales is search,
and the best algorithms we have for SMT are the ones that reduce to SAT.
I ache a little at the beauty this leaves behind, interpolation above all,
but mechanizing these algorithms is within reach of today's proof assistants,
and foundational proof and push-button automation need not be at odds.
I hope these reductions lay the groundwork for verification efforts of far greater ambition.
