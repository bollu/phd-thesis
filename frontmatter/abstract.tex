\epigraph{Immortality would have been meaningless, trapped in a ``machine'' with a finite number of possible states; in a finite time he would have exhausted the list of every possible thing he could be"}{Greg Egan, Permutation City}

A real computer is a finite thing: finitely many registers, each of finite width.
The set of all computers is not.
A compiler writer reasons not about one machine but about a family of them,
and so the objects they must get right---a peephole rewrite that has to hold at \emph{every}
register width, a floating-point operation whose meaning is given over the reals---live in
infinite domains, even though every instance of them is finite.
This thesis is about closing that gap where it matters most for compilers, hardware, and
cryptography: the theories of bitvectors and floating-point arithmetic.

Automating such reasoning today forces a choice.
SMT solvers decide the finite instances with formidable speed,
but are large, aggressively optimized, unverified programs.
Interactive theorem provers check every step against a small kernel,
but have traditionally offered only weak automation for finite-domain theories.
We want the speed without surrendering the guarantee.

Our key idea is borrowed from the mathematician's notion of compactness:
an infinite object can often be understood by reasoning about finitely much of it.
We create, mechanize, and exploit reductions from the infinite to the finite---from a family
of machines to a single machine, from the reals to the finitely many bits that survive
rounding---and discharge what remains by SAT solving, inside the Lean proof assistant.
The appeal is that with finiteness comes tractability, with tractability automation,
and with automation scale; and that the reductions are themselves mathematical content,
which is precisely what a proof assistant is good at holding.

We first formalize a core calculus for SSA-based compiler intermediate representations,
generic over the compiler IR itself, and prove correct a generic peephole rewriter,
together with proof automation that eliminates the abstraction overhead of the calculus
and case studies ranging from LLVM bitvector rewrites to fully homomorphic encryption.
Discharging such rewrites by hand is tedious,
and it is this tedium that drives the demand for the push-button automation that follows.

For a width-parametric rewrite, the infinity is the width.
We show that with a single symbolic width the problem reduces to reachability in a finite
automaton, and we mechanize the model-checking algorithms---$k$-induction, reachability,
emptiness, minimization---that turn this into scalable, certified tactics.
We then lift the mono-width restriction: encoding symbolic widths as bitmasks,
any multi-width parametric formula reduces with only linear blowup to an equisatisfiable
mono-width one. This yields a new decidable fragment of the multi-width theory,
and replaces exponential enumeration over widths with a single fixed-width query.

For floating point, the infinity is that of the reals,
and rounding is what buys back finiteness.
We mechanize the SMT-LIB theory of floating point,
build verified bitblasting circuits for its operations,
and obtain the first verified floating-point bitblaster,
which runs within a constant geometric-mean factor (around $33\times$) of the
state-of-the-art unverified solver Bitwuzla,
while offering end-to-end correctness guarantees and supporting the small formats used in
machine-learning quantization.

What we learn is that the price of foundational trust is real, but affordable where it is
actually charged.
On the largest problems we throw at them, our mechanized solvers remain one to two orders
of magnitude slower than the unverified state of the art;
but the problems that arise in peephole rewrite verification are small enough that this
gap does not bite.
Foundational proof and push-button automation need not be at odds.
We hope that these reductions, and their mechanizations,
lay the groundwork for verification efforts of far greater ambition.
