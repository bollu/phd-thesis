Automated reasoning over finite domains underpins the verification of compilers, hardware, and cryptography.
SMT solvers decide such theories efficiently, but they are large, unverified programs.
In contrast, interactive theorem provers (ITPs) offer foundational guarantees, but have traditionally
lacked the automation that makes finite-domain reasoning practical.
This thesis narrows that gap: it contributes \emph{mechanized} old and new decision procedures for finite-domain theories,
implemented and proved correct in the Lean proof assistant.

Toward grounding our work in practical applications, we focus on the theory of bitvectors and floating-point arithmetic,
since they are the bedrock of what a compiler emits. We first formalize a core calculus for SSA-based compiler intermediate
representations, generic over the compiler IR itself.
Our mechanization provides a verified peephole rewriter,
proof automation that eliminates the abstraction overhead of the SSA calculus,
and case studies ranging from LLVM bitvector rewrites to fully homomorphic encryption.

Verifying such rewrites is tedious, and drives a demand for push-button bitvector automation.
We contribute mechanized decision procedures for \emph{width-independent} bitvector predicates,
viewing classical algorithms in this space through the lens of model checking,
and generalizing them along multiple axes.
Firstly, when there is a single symbolic width, we show that the problem reduces to a finite-state automata reachability problem,
and that the automata based model checking algorithms can be used to obtain scalable,
certified tactics.

We then generalize from the mono-width to the multi-width setting, where we prove that
any multi-width parametric bitvector formula reduces to an equisatisfiable mono-width formula with only linear blowup.
This relies on an encoding symbolic widths as bitmasks.
This reduction not only yields a decidable fragment of multi-width parametric bitvector theory,
but also yields solver for the bounded-width fragment that replaces exponential enumeration with a single fixed-width query.
This solver is both algorithmically interesting, and is fast in practice.

Finally, we extend mechanized bitblasting from bitvectors to floating-point arithmetic.
We mechanize the SMT-LIB theory of floating point,
build verified bitblasting circuits for its operations,
and obtain the first verified floating-point bitblaster,
which runs within a constant geometric-mean factor ($\~33\times$) of the state-of-the-art unverified solver Bitwuzla,
while offering end-to-end correctness guarantees and supporting the small formats used in machine-learning quantization.

With this work we show that the price of foundational trust in finite-domain is not too dear,
and we outline a path toward making the price of foundational trust in finite-domain reasoning even cheaper.
