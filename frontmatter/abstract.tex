\epigraph{Immortality would have been meaningless, trapped in a ``machine'' with a finite number of possible states; in a finite time he would have exhausted the list of every possible thing he could be}{Greg Egan, Permutation City}

As computer scientists, we often contemplate idealizations of computers that are infinite in multiple dimensions: infinite memory, infinite registers, infinite precision.
However, real computer is a finite thing: finitely many registers, finitely much memory, finitely many bits of precision.
Thus, in principle, everything about a real computer can be exhaustive enumerated, and all questions can be answered by a brute force search.
But things are never so easy. When we write a compiler, we reason not about one, finite, machine but about an infinite family of machines,
parametrized by the dimensions of the machine that can be scaled up or down.
Thus, for example, a rewrite has to be correct at \emph{every} register width,
a floating-point rewrite has to hold at \emph{every} exponent and manitssa combination.
Can we do better, and somehow reduce these infinitary problems to finite ones, to exploit the speed of brute-force search and automation?
In this these, we make an attempt at translating some of these infinite problems
into a (family) of finite ones, and proving these translations correct
in order to enable push-button automation via search.

% Automating such reasoning today forces a choice.
% SMT solvers decide the finite instances with formidable speed,
% but are large, aggressively optimized, unverified programs.
% Interactive theorem provers check every step against a small kernel,
% but have traditionally offered only weak automation for finite-domain theories.
% We want the speed without surrendering the guarantee.

Our key idea is borrowed from the mathematician's notion of compactness:
an infinite object can often be understood by reasoning about finitely much of it.
We create, mechanize, and exploit reductions from the infinite to the finite.
We discharge what remains of the finite portion by SAT solving.
With finiteness comes tractability, with tractability automation,
and with automation scale.


Toward this goal of large-scale compiler verification,
we first formalize a core calculus for SSA-based compiler intermediate representations,
generic over the compiler IR itself, and prove correct a generic peephole rewriter,
together with proof automation that eliminates the abstraction overhead of the calculus
and case studies ranging from LLVM bitvector rewrites to fully homomorphic encryption.
Discharging such rewrites by hand is tedious,
and it is this tedium that drives the demand for the push-button automation that follows.

Next, to enable large-scale verifcation of integer bitvector rewrites,
we prove that bitvector problems with a single symbolic width the problem reduces to reachability in a
finite automaton. This enables us to exploit finiteness,
and use (finite!) state-space exploration based model-checking algorithms such as
$k$-induction and regular language emptiness, to effectively decide these problems.
As usual, the bedrock is SAT solving, where $k$-induction reduces to a large SAT query.
Next, we lift the mono-width restriction, and by a neat encoding trick,
prove that any multi-width parametric formula reduces to an equisatisfiable mono-width formula.
This yields a new sound and complete fragment of the multi-width theory,
and replaces exponential enumeration over widths with a single fixed-width query.

For floating point, while the semantics are defined in terms of real numbers,
rounding back to a fixed width buys back finiteness.
We mechanize the SMT-LIB theory of floating point, build verified bitblasting circuits for its operations,
and obtain floating-point bitblaster that's trustworthy across all widths and exponent and mantissa combinations.
We are 33x slower than the state-of-the-art unverified solver Bitwuzla,
but in exchange, offer end-to-end correctness guarantees.

Overall, what we learn is a flavour of the bitter lesson, where indeed, the only thing that scales is search and automation.
Furthermore, we learn that mechanization of such algorithms is not out of reach of today's proof assistant technology.
On the largest problems we throw at them, our mechanized solvers remain one to two orders
of magnitude slower than the unverified state of the art. 
However, the problems that arise in peephole rewrite verification are small enough that this gap does not bite.
Overall, foundational proof and push-button automation need not be at odds.
We hope that these reductions, and their mechanizations lay the groundwork for verification efforts of far greater ambition.
