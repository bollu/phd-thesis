\epigraph{Civilization advances by extending the number of important operations which we can perform without thinking about them}{Alfred North Whitehead, An Introduction to Mathematics}

\lettrine{A} compiler, conceptually, is a pretty simple program:
it translates a program in one language into an equivalent program in another one.
However, a production-grade compiler is enormously complex.
For example, in the LLVM compiler framework, the peephole rewriter is a conceptually simple program,
that scans for a small window of instructions and replaces them with a more efficient equivalent sequence of instructions.
However, in terms of the actual implementation, the peephole rewrite is enormous,
and is about 10\% of the entire LLVM codebase.
To tame this complexity, we would like to verify the correctness of LLVM,
since the correctness of any program compiled by LLVM depends on the correctness of the compiler itself.
Clearly, verifying all of LLVM is not doable by a single person,
so we choose to focus on the peephole rewriter, which is a large, critical part of LLVM,
but is crucially modular. Each transformation is small enough to prove correct,
and the rewriter as a whole is built of thousands of such proof obligations.
These are far more than can be discharged by hand.
This volume eventually demands push-button automation for proving the correctness of each rewrite.

In this thesis, we first formalize a core calculus for SSA-based compiler intermediate representations,
generic over the user's IR, and prove a generic peephole rewriter correct,
with case studies ranging from fully homomorphic encryption to LLVM's bitvector rewrites.
The calculus supplies the structural half of the problem, i.e., manipulating SSA, for free.
The arithmetic half remains, since each rewrite bottoms out in a  mathematical identity that must be discharged automatically.

In the second half of the thesis, we introduce new, formally verified tools for reasoning about bitvectors and floating-point numbers,
which discharges the arithmetic half of the problem.
The decision procedures we build rest on bitblasting.
We build on \bvdecide{}, Lean's fixed-width bitblaster,
which carries a bitvector goal all the way down to a SAT instance, solves it,
and has the resulting certificate rechecked by Lean's kernel.
Its design deliberately mirrors Bitwuzla,
a state-of-the-art solver for fixed-width bitvectors, floating point, and arrays,
and it is the bedrock of the rest of the thesis.

Perhaps counterintuitively, the proof obligations a compiler hands us are not immediately bitblastable.
For example, a rewrite has to be correct at \emph{every} register width, whereas a bitblaster wants one fixed width,
and the standard remedy (used, for example, by the Alive peephole rewrite verifier) is to enumerate the widths and bitblast each one separately.
We show that this is unnecessary. Predicates that must hold at all widths reduce to reachability in a finite automaton,
which we decide by $k$-induction and language emptiness.
Next, by an encoding trick that writes widths as bitmasks,
any formula over $n$ symbolic widths reduces to an equisatisfiable single-width one.
This trades an exponential number of solver queries for a \emph{single} query,
and carves out a new sound and complete fragment of the multi-width theory.
Bitblasting, it turns out, can even reach parametric bitvector theory.

Floating point is one of the more intricate pieces of SMT-LIB theory missing from a fully verified Bitwuzla.
Following SymFPU, a state-of-the-art floating-point bitblaster,
which showed in a sequence of papers that bitblasting decisively outcompetes the alternatives for floating-point SMT solving,
we mechanize the SMT-LIB \qffp{} theory and build verified bitblasting circuits for almost all of its operations.
Moreover, we prove the correctness of these circuits in Lean,
thereby eliminating various trust issues around nonstandard floating-point formats and rounding modes.

Overall, our width-generic solvers prove more all-width problems than the unverified state of the art,
and our floating-point bitblaster, while slower than Bitwuzla,
offers in exchange end-to-end guarantees down to Lean's kernel.
Foundational proof and push-button automation need not be at odds,
and we hope the reductions developed here lay the groundwork for verification efforts of far greater ambition.
