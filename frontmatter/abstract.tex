\epigraph{Civilization advances by extending the number of important operations which we can perform without thinking about them}{Alfred North Whitehead, An Introduction to Mathematics}

A compiler is a large program.
In LLVM, the peephole rewriter alone is larger than the entire vectorizer!
This thesis is about verifying such a compiler.
Its unit of work is the peephole rewrite: a single, local transformation on a finite window of instructions.
Each is small enough to prove correct, but compiler correctness is,
to a first approximation, thousands of such little theorems.

Can we prove them by hand? In principle, yes. In practice, this is deathly tedious.
We first formalize a core calculus for SSA-based compiler intermediate representations,
generic over the user's IR, and prove correct a generic peephole rewriter,
with case studies from LLVM bitvector rewrites to fully homomorphic encryption.
This teaches us where the difficulty lives.
The structural half of each proof comes for free;
the arithmetic half does not, and it is here that our LLVM case study stalls.
It is this tedium that drives the demand for push-button automation: a decision procedure.

Which automation should we bet on? Bitblasting.
Encode a rich theory into propositional logic, and hand the result to a SAT solver.
This tradition powers today's fastest SMT solvers,
and has recently been made foundational:
\bvdecide{} bitblasts fixed-width bitvector goals inside the Lean proof assistant,
and has the SAT certificate rechecked by Lean's kernel.
Speed and trust, at the same time. We take this as our bedrock.

But things are never so easy.
The obligations a compiler hands us are not bitblastable as they stand.
A rewrite has to be correct at \emph{every} register width, where a bitblaster wants one fixed width,
and floating-point semantics live over the real numbers, which admit no bit-level encoding.
The heart of this thesis is three reductions that close this gap, each mechanized in Lean.
First, predicates that must hold at all widths reduce to reachability in a finite automaton,
which we decide by $k$-induction and language emptiness, bottoming out, as usual, in SAT.
Next, by a neat encoding trick, widths as bitmasks,
any formula over many symbolic widths reduces, with linear blowup, to an equisatisfiable single-width one.
This trades exponential width enumeration for one fixed-width query,
and yields a new sound and complete fragment of the multi-width theory.
Finally, we mechanize the SMT-LIB \qffp{} theory and build verified bitblasting circuits for its operations,
trustworthy at every exponent and mantissa width.

Does the bet pay off? We think so.
Our width-generic solvers prove more all-width problems than the unverified state of the art,
and our floating-point bitblaster, while slower than Bitwuzla,
offers end-to-end guarantees down to Lean's kernel in exchange.
The price of verification is a constant factor, not an asymptotic one,
and on peephole-sized problems that factor does not bite.
Overall, what we learn is a flavour of the bitter lesson: the only thing that scales is search.
Mechanizing such algorithms is within reach of today's proof assistants,
and foundational proof and push-button automation need not be at odds.
We hope these reductions lay the groundwork for verification efforts of far greater ambition.
