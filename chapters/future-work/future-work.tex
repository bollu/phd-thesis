\label{ch:future-work}

\lettrine{T}{his} thesis set out to show that decision procedures for
finite-domain theories can be mechanized---implemented, proved correct, and run
inside a proof assistant at a bounded, size-independent constant factor over the
unverified state of the art, rather than the asymptotic blow-up usually assumed
of foundational methods. It made the case along a single arc. \Cref{ch:lean-mlir} built a
framework for verified peephole rewriting over SSA-based IRs; by making the
structural part of a rewrite's proof essentially free, it isolates the
finite-domain proof obligations that each rewrite leaves behind. The remaining
chapters discharged those obligations with mechanized decision procedures of
growing generality. \Cref{ch:mono-width} decided width-independent bitvector
predicates for the mono-width case---viewing classical algorithms
through the lens of model checking---and \cref{ch:multi-width} then generalized,
by encoding symbolic widths as bitmasks, to the multi-width case.
\Cref{ch:floating-point} carried
the same verified-bitblasting idea from bitvectors to floating-point arithmetic,
yielding the first verified floating-point bitblaster.

Each artefact is mechanized in Lean, evaluated against the unverified state of
the art, and runs within a small factor of it while offering end-to-end
correctness guarantees. Together they are evidence that foundational proof and
push-button automation need not be at odds: the finite-domain reasoning that
underpins compilers, hardware, and cryptography can be carried out inside a
proof assistant, at scale, with the answers trusted down to the kernel.

\section{The Price of Verification}

It is worth stating the price of this trust plainly, because it is not zero.
None of the mechanized procedures matches the raw speed of the unverified state
of the art; each runs within a bounded, size-independent constant factor of
it---roughly an order of magnitude for the bitvector core, and a geometric-mean
factor of about $33\times$ for floating point. The verified $k$-induction
backend of the width-independent solver is, moreover, paired with an
\emph{unverified} model checker (rIC3) precisely because, on the hardest
unbounded instances, the unverified tool is faster. The claim of this thesis is
therefore not that verification is free, nor even that its cost is always small,
but that the cost is a \emph{bounded, predictable constant}---independent of
problem size---rather than the asymptotic blow-up that foundational methods are
usually assumed to carry; and that, for automation whose every answer is trusted
down to the kernel, this is a price worth paying.

The shape of that constant is what matters, and it is worth separating from its
magnitude. A factor of $33\times$ is not a small number. But it does not grow
with the width of a bitvector, the size of a formula, or the number of symbolic
widths in play; it is the fixed overhead of computing inside a kernel-checked
reflection procedure rather than in optimized C++. The alternative that
foundational methods are usually assumed to force---enumerate the widths,
enumerate the values---is not a constant at all. Where this thesis competes
against such an alternative directly, the comparison is not close: the bounded
multi-width solver replaces exponentially many \qfbv{} calls with one, and on
small floating-point formats our bitblaster beats exhaustive enumeration by
\SmallFpTimeElapsedRatioOKernel{} in geomean, and by \SmallFpTimeoutSpeedupLB{}
on the instances enumeration cannot finish at all. Verification costs a constant;
the enumeration it displaces costs an exponent.

Was re-derivation the right bet? \Cref{sec:intro:trust} set out the stance: rather
than run an unverified solver and check its certificate, port the algorithms into
the proof assistant and prove them correct. The discipline turned out to be
productive beyond its stated aim. Mechanizing an algorithm forces every
assumption in it into the open, and that pressure produced results that were not
on the agenda---the width-as-bitmask reduction of \cref{ch:multi-width}, the
reverse-BFS argument for first-UIP computation in conflict analysis, and, in
\cref{ch:floating-point}, the discovery that SymFPU's unpacker and rounder carry
undocumented width assumptions that make them abort on exactly the FP8 formats
machine learning has come to depend on. A certificate-checking approach would
have inherited those gaps rather than found them: one cannot check a certificate
for an answer the underlying tool declines to produce. The bet also has a bill,
and it is paid in proof effort---three \qffp{} operations remain bitblasted but
unproven, and the fast paths that make SymFPU quick remain unported.

What resisted us was almost never the high-level algorithm and almost always
the arithmetic beneath it. The classical procedures we set out to mechanize---%
bitblasting circuits, automata constructions, $k$-induction---are, in their
essential ideas, well understood; porting them into Lean to inherit both their
power and a kernel-checked correctness proof proved a productive discipline, and
in places it forced small original results of its own (the reverse-BFS argument
for first-UIP computation in conflict analysis; the width-as-bitmask reduction
of \cref{ch:multi-width}). The obstacle, everywhere, was \emph{nonlinear}
reasoning: obligations mixing multiplication of variables, integer division,
modular arithmetic, and powers of two with symbolic exponents, for which no
automation exists and which therefore come to dominate the proof effort with
bookkeeping that is conceptually trivial yet mechanically exhausting. If a
single lesson generalizes from these chapters, it is that effective proof
automation for nonlinear arithmetic---ideally uniform across \texttt{Nat},
\texttt{Int}, \texttt{Rat}, and \texttt{BitVec}---is the missing enabling
technology for verified finite-domain reasoning at scale. It is also the thread
that ties this retrospective to the directions that follow.

The techniques of this thesis also open several concrete directions, adapted from
the research proposals in my second-year report. Each takes up a limitation left
standing by an earlier chapter: the fragment boundary that bounds the solvers of
\cref{ch:mono-width,ch:multi-width}, and the theory coverage and error analysis
that bound the floating-point bitblaster of \cref{ch:floating-point}. We begin
with those we consider most fertile---each backed by a concrete unpublished
result or algorithmic plan---and close with briefer sketches and a broader
outlook.

\section{Nonlinear Bitvector Arithmetic via the 2-adic Integers}
\label{sec:fw:2adic}

The solvers of \cref{ch:mono-width,ch:multi-width} decide the
\emph{linear-bitwise} fragment of the parametric bitvector theory. We have an
unpublished result showing that \emph{nonlinear} arithmetic in the parametric
bitvector theory is decidable, by a reduction to the first-order theory of
the 2-adic integers. On the other hand, our unpublished results also show
that integrating \emph{any} bitwise operation into such a theory immediately
leads to undecidability.\footnote{%
\url{https://pixel-druid.com/articles/non-linear-theory-of-2-adics-does-not-mix-with-bitwise-operations}}
This implies that the nonlinear arithmetic fragment of the parametric
bitvector theory is (maximally) decidable: to write incomplete solvers for
the full theory, one must first build a complete solver for the nonlinear
arithmetic fragment, which can then be mixed with bitwise operations via
theory combination.

Toward this, we propose implementing a fast cylindrical algebraic
decomposition (CAD) solver for the 2-adics. While the decidability of the
full first-order theory of the 2-adics has been known in theory since the
1960s~\citep{ax1966diophantine}, that result was not algorithmic. A CAD-style
algorithm based on cell decomposition does
exist~\citep{quantifier-elimination-padic}, but, to the best of our
knowledge, no implementation of it does. We therefore propose to implement
such a solver in C++ or Rust, leveraging existing libraries such as
FLINT~\citep{hart2013flint} for polynomial manipulation and root finding.
This also sets the stage for a verified reimplementation in Lean, extending
the reach of the verified solvers of this thesis beyond the linear-bitwise
fragment.

\section{Ackermannization, Incremental Solving, and the Theory of Arrays}
\label{sec:fw:ackermannization}

Adding \emph{incremental solving} to \bvdecide{} would enable much better
variants of ackermannization, thereby unlocking support for the theory of
arrays. Together with the floating-point support of \cref{ch:floating-point},
this would bring the verified solver to theory parity with
Bitwuzla~\citep{niemetz2023bitwuzla}
($\qfbv{} + \qffp{} + \qfuf{}$), which suffices to model
almost all behaviour of realistic production languages such as C and C++.
Industry formalization efforts would be a natural testing ground for
evaluating whether such an extended solver can handle real verification
workloads. Bitwuzla's ackermannization and multiplication-abstraction
machinery is a natural starting point for a mechanization.

\section{Average-Case Floating-Point Error Analysis via \texorpdfstring{$\delta$}{delta}-Satisfiability}
\label{sec:fw:deltasat}

Worst-case floating-point error bounds are a key ingredient for optimizing
machine-learning kernels, yet they are often too pessimistic for
machine-learning workloads. We propose to compute tighter,
\emph{average-case} error bounds by building on the dReal
solver~\citep{gao2013dreal}, which decides \emph{bounded} quantified problems
over the reals. The key idea of dReal is that for Lipschitz-continuous
functions over bounded domains, one can compute a (uniform) discretization of
the domain that is sufficiently fine to decide satisfiability up to a given
$\delta$; this is $\delta$-satisfiability, solved by a CDCL-style algorithm.
The key insight for our proposal is that average-case pointwise error
analysis for machine-learning kernels reduces to bounded quantified formulas
over the reals extended with integration. A recent solver,
$\int$dreal~\citep{rivera2025checking}, supports exactly this theory, and we
plan to adapt it to floating-point error analysis.

\section{Broader Outlook: AI for Formal Verification}
\label{sec:fw:ai}

Beyond decision procedures themselves, two directions apply the
infrastructure of this thesis to machine-assisted formalization.

\paragraph{Large-scale auto-formalization of software engineering theories.}
Formal libraries for bitvector and floating-point theories remain sparse,
owing to a lack of both training data (such as textbooks) and domain-specific
tactics that work well over these rich domains. The tactic infrastructure and
domain knowledge developed in this thesis provide the foundation needed to
bootstrap large-scale formalization efforts for these theories, given
sufficient models and compute.

\paragraph{Teaching models to metaprogram in Lean.}
A principled tactic in Lean comprises (a)~a decision procedure for the
domain, and (b)~the metaprogramming glue connecting it to the user frontend
(proof by reflection, \texttt{simp}, \texttt{grind}, simprocs, and so on).
The metaprogramming API is complex, underdocumented, and contains subtle
invariants that are easy to break; if AI models are to create their own
tactics, they must master this metaprogramming layer. We would like to
explore whether cleaner abstractions---analogous to how \texttt{SymM} inside
\texttt{GrindM} abstracts away the metaprogramming machinery for symbolic
simulation---can be designed for proof by reflection, making the API
tractable for both humans and models.

\section*{Closing}

The practitioner who opened this thesis was made to choose between speed without
a warranty and a warranty without speed. That choice is narrower than it was.
Compilers, hardware, and cryptography rest on finite-domain reasoning, and the
procedures that discharge it need not be taken on trust: they can be implemented,
proved correct, and run inside a proof assistant, on the problems practitioners
actually have, at a cost that can be stated in advance. With this work we show
that the price of foundational trust in finite-domain reasoning is a bounded,
size-independent constant, rather than the asymptotic penalty that verified
automation is commonly assumed to carry.
