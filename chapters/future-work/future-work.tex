\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:future-work}
\epigraph{The achievement we celebrate today is but a step, an opening of opportunity, to the greater triumphs and achievements that await us}{Jawaharlal Nehru, Tryst with Destiny}

\lettrine{T}{his} thesis set out to show that decision procedures for
finite-domain theories can be mechanized to be fast enough to be usable on real problems.
Each of our solvers is implemented in Lean, and all of them are proof producing in some fashion.
They are evaluated against the unverified state of the art, and none of them matches its raw speed.
Our floating-point bitblaster trails Bitwuzla by a geometric-mean factor of $\SmtlibRandSpeedupBitwuzlaOverOurs\times$,
and the fixed-width bitvector engine it is built on is itself within roughly an order of magnitude of Bitwuzla's.
What we buy at that price is an end-to-end correctness guarantee that reaches down to Lean's kernel.
The price is a constant factor rather than an asymptotic one:
it does not grow with the size of the problem or with the number of symbolic widths,
where the enumerative approaches we replace blow up exponentially.
Together, our solvers are evidence that foundational proof and push-button automation need not be at odds:
The finite-domain reasoning that underpins compilers, hardware, and cryptography can be carried out inside a proof assistant at scale.

Was UNSAT certificates the right bet? It might have been,
since it enabled us to focus on ``what mattered'', which are the high-level algorithms and their correctness proofs.

I also indicate future directions for this work,
which include extending the verified solvers to support more theories,
and using the infrastructure of this thesis to bootstrap large-scale formalization efforts in Lean.

\section{Fullly Mechanized Bitwuzla}


The key missing piece of the verified solver is support for the theory of arrays.
Arrays can be implemented in terms of a larger body of theory, called \emph{ackermannization},
which reduces array reasoning to uninterpreted functions and bitvectors.
The idea is that in order to... arrays, we do ... uninterpreted functions.
Uninterpreted functions on a finite domain are captured entirely by their congruece rule,
$x = y \implies f(x) = f(y)$. So, we creae new atoms called $f_x$ and $f_y$, which stand in for $f(x)$ and $f(y)$, and add the axiom
$x = y \implies f_x = f_y$. This is a standard technique for adding support for uninterpreted functions to a SAT based theory.
However, since there are quadratically many pairs of terms,
doing this eagerly is not feasible.
Instead, one can do this \emph{incrementally}, only adding the axiom when the solver finds a counterexample to it.

Adding \emph{incremental solving} to \bvdecide{} would therefore enable much better
variants of ackermannization, and unlocking support for the theory of
arrays. Together with the floating-point support of \cref{ch:floating-point},
this would bring the verified solver to theory parity with
Bitwuzla~\citep{niemetz2023bitwuzla} ($\qfbv{} + \qffp{} + \qfuf{}$),
which suffices to model almost all behaviour of realistic production languages such as C and C++.
Industry formalization efforts would be a natural testing ground for
evaluating whether such an extended solver can handle real verification
workloads. Bitwuzla's ackermannization and multiplication-abstraction
machinery is a natural starting point for a mechanization.

\section{Tensor Bitblasting}

Tensor bitblasting should be possible, since all the parts are there:
fpsan for floating point level equivalence,
tensorright for shape level equivalence,
linear layouts for layout encodings.
This builds exactly along our strengths: fpsan can be implemented as a bitvector solver,
and guarantees equivalence-upto-exponential ring, which is what ML practicioners care about.
tensorright offers a guarnatee similar to our automata based solvers,
where they compute a safety cutoff for the number of dimensions to be checked for,
and they then check upto this dimension size. The key innovation will be to find a good BV based encoding for this,
but it should be possible, using similar masking tricks as our multi-width solver.
Finally, linear layouts shows how to encode layouts efficiently in terms of bitpatterns,
under the assumption that the layouts are all going to be powers of 2, which they are in practice.
This should feed into tensorright, and allow us to implement a tensor bitblaster that
is fast (since it is powered by SAT), as well as providing a bounded-to-unbounded transfer principle,
by building on top of tensorright's theory.


\section*{Closing}

Compilers, hardware, and cryptography rest on finite-domain reasoning, and the
procedures that discharge it need not be taken on trust.
They can be implemented, proved correct, and run inside a proof assistant,
on the problems practitioners actually have.
With this work we show that the price of foundational trust in not too steep to pay.
