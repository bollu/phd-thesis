\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:intro}
\epigraph{Begin at the beginning, and go on till you come to the end: then
stop.}{The King, \emph{Alice's Adventures in Wonderland}}

\lettrine{C}{ompilers}, hardware designs, and cryptographic routines are
trusted by billions of users, yet the reasoning that justifies their
correctness ultimately bottoms out in \emph{finite domains}: machine integers
of a fixed width, registers whose width is a design parameter, and
floating-point numbers laid out bit by bit in IEEE-754 formats. Two research
communities have built impressive---but largely disjoint---tools for this kind
of reasoning. Automated SMT solvers such as Bitwuzla~\citep{niemetz2023bitwuzla}
and cvc5~\citep{barbosa2022cvc5} decide finite-domain theories with remarkable
efficiency, but are themselves large, highly optimized, and unverified
programs. Interactive theorem provers (ITPs) such as
Lean~\citep{demoura2021lean} offer foundational guarantees, checked by a small
trusted kernel, but have historically provided little of the push-button
automation that makes finite-domain reasoning practical at scale.

This thesis contributes to closing that gap. It began as an effort in verified
compilation, but the binding constraint proved to lie elsewhere: a verified
compiler must discharge \emph{thousands} of proof obligations over bitvectors
and floating-point values, and it is these arithmetic obligations---not the
structural transformations that surround them---that resist mechanization at
scale. That discovery redirected the work reported here toward the decision
procedures that discharge such obligations, and toward a single question asked
in four parts: whether peephole rewriting over realistic SSA-based IRs can be
verified \emph{generically}, so that the structural proof burden is paid once
and for all (\cref{ch:lean-mlir}); whether width-independent bitvector
predicates admit \emph{verified, proof-producing} decision procedures, and
whether these scale from the mono-width to the multi-width setting
(\cref{ch:mono-width,ch:multi-width});
whether floating-point bitblasting---the unverified heart of SMT
floating-point solving---can itself be mechanized and proved correct
(\cref{ch:floating-point}); and, cutting across all three, whether verified
automation can keep pace with the fastest unverified solvers in practice, and
not merely in principle.

The central claim is that
\emph{decision procedures for finite-domain theories can be mechanized---%
implemented, proved correct, and run inside a proof assistant at a small,
constant-factor cost over the unverified state of the art, rather than the
order-of-magnitude slowdown usually assumed of foundational methods}. We support this
claim with a sequence of mechanized artefacts in Lean, each published and
evaluated against the fastest unverified tools, and each extending the reach of
verified automation: from fixed widths, to mono-width, to multi-width, and
finally to floating-point arithmetic.

\section{Automated Speed and Interactive Trust}

The tension at the heart of this thesis is one of \emph{trust}. On one side
stand automated SMT solvers---Bitwuzla~\citep{niemetz2023bitwuzla},
cvc5~\citep{barbosa2022cvc5}, Z3~\citep{de2008z3}---which decide finite-domain
theories with formidable speed and on which compiler writers and hardware
verifiers rely daily. Yet these solvers are large, aggressively optimized
programs whose correctness rests on manual reasoning and testing, and they are
not infallible: a single random-testing campaign aimed at floating-point
solvers uncovered soundness or completeness bugs in \emph{every} SMT-LIB
floating-point solver to which it was applied~\citep{brain2019building}. On the
other side stand interactive theorem provers such as
Lean~\citep{demoura2021lean}, Rocq, and Isabelle, whose every theorem is
checked by a small trusted kernel, but which have historically offered only
weak, best-effort automation for these theories. Isabelle's
\emph{Sledgehammer}~\citep{blanchette2011automatic} delegates goals to external
provers, yet its bitvector reach is limited precisely because the underlying
solvers expose no fine-grained, checkable proof. The practitioner is thus made
to choose between speed without a warranty and a warranty without speed.

This thesis descends from, and departs from, the verified-compilation
tradition. CompCert~\citep{leroy2016compcert} showed that an optimizing C
compiler can be proved correct end to end, and Vellvm~\citep{zhao2012formalizing}
carried the same ambition to LLVM's IR. At the granularity of individual
rewrites, the Alive project~\citep{lopes2015provably} and its successor
Alive2~\citep{lopes2021alive2} brought SMT-based translation validation into the
day-to-day of LLVM development. But these tools inherit the trust profile of the
solvers beneath them: Alive2 is itself an unverified program, and---lacking
parametric bitvectors---it validates a width-generic rewrite by
\emph{enumerating} concrete widths up to a fixed bound, which is both expensive
and unsound above that bound. The lesson we draw is that verifying the compiler
does not suffice if the automation that discharges its arithmetic obligations
is itself unverified.

Several lines of work already try to bridge automated speed and kernel-checked
trust, and it helps to place this thesis among them. \emph{Certifying}
approaches run an external solver and check the certificate it emits inside the
kernel: SMTCoq~\citep{ekici2017smtcoq} reconstructs proofs from SMT solvers in
Rocq, and CoqQFBV~\citep{shi2021coqqfbv} checks a bitblaster's SAT certificate;
systems such as F\textsuperscript{*}/HACL\textsuperscript{*}~\citep{swamy2023proof}
couple strong SMT automation to verified code. A parallel tradition mechanizes
floating-point arithmetic itself---Harrison's HOL~Light
development~\citep{harrison1999machine}, Flocq~\citep{boldo2011flocq}, and
VCFloat~\citep{appel2022vcfloat}---but proves floating-point \emph{theorems}
rather than verifying a floating-point \emph{solver}. The stance taken here is
complementary to both: rather than trust an external solver and check its
output, we \emph{re-derive the solver inside the proof assistant}. We port the
algorithms of the state of the art---Bitwuzla's bitblasting and SymFPU's
floating-point circuits~\citep{symfpu}---into Lean and prove them correct, so
that each answer is trusted down to the kernel, justified by proof-by-reflection
and, for the SAT core, an LRAT certificate, rather than by the authority of the
tool that produced it. In the vocabulary of a working compiler engineer, the
aim is verified versions of the tools the LLVM community already relies on.

\section{Motivation: Verified Rewriting Demands Verified Automation}

Our starting point is compilation. A modern optimizing compiler is not one
monolithic transformation but an accretion of \emph{thousands} of small, local
rewrites: LLVM's InstCombine peephole optimizer alone makes up roughly a tenth
of all IR-transforming code in the project. Each rewrite is individually
simple, yet they coalesce into the whole, and the compiler is correct only if
\emph{every} one of them is. Frameworks such as MLIR~\citep{lattner2020mlir}
multiply this further, encouraging many domain-specific IRs, each with its own
family of peephole rewrites.

Verifying rewrites at this scale in an ITP---checking each proof once and for
all, rather than revalidating it on every compilation as SMT-based tools
do~\citep{lopes2021alive2}---makes two demands at once, and they are the two
halves of a single workflow. First, we need a way to \emph{state and compose} rewrites over an
arbitrary IR without re-proving the structural plumbing---SSA form, dominance,
nested regions---for each one. Second, every rewrite still discharges to a
proof obligation over finite-domain values, and doing these by hand does not
scale: a single InstCombine rewrite may require pages of bit-level reasoning.
The framework we present in \cref{ch:lean-mlir} answers the first demand,
making the \emph{structural} part of such proofs essentially free; in doing so
it isolates the remaining \emph{semantic} obligations---bitvector and
floating-point goals---as the true bottleneck. The rest of the thesis builds
the verified automation that discharges them.

\section{Contributions}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A verified framework for peephole rewriting in SSA-based IRs}
  (\cref{ch:lean-mlir}). We formalize a core calculus for SSA-based IRs,
  generic over the user's IR and covering MLIR-style regions, mechanize it in
  Lean with an MLIR-syntax frontend, and prove correct a generic peephole
  rewriter as well as dead-code and common-subexpression elimination.
  Published at ITP~2024~\citep{leanmlir}.

  \item \textbf{Certified decision procedures for width-independent bitvector
  predicates} (\cref{ch:mono-width}). We mechanize key model-checking
  algorithms---$k$-induction, automata reachability, emptiness, and
  minimization---and use them to build scalable, certified tactics for
  bitvector predicates that hold at \emph{all} widths, plus a specialized
  solver for mixed Boolean-arithmetic. Published at
  OOPSLA~2025~\citep{bhat2025certified}.

  \item \textbf{Sound and complete solving for multi-width parametric
  bitvectors} (\cref{ch:multi-width}). We prove that multi-width formulas
  over $n$ symbolic widths reduce, with linear blowup, to an equisatisfiable
  mono-width formula by encoding widths as bitmasks; this collapses
  exponential enumeration into one fixed-width solver call and yields a new
  decidable fragment subsuming the prior state of the art. Conditionally
  accepted at OOPSLA~2026~\citep{bhat2026multiwidth}.

  \item \textbf{Mechanized floating-point bitblasting}
  (\cref{ch:floating-point}). We mechanize the SMT-LIB \qffp{} theory, build
  verified bitblasting circuits for its operations, and obtain the first
  verified floating-point bitblaster, evaluated on SMT-COMP and on new
  small-format (E4M3/E5M2) benchmarks. In submission~\citep{bhat2027floatingpoint}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Thesis Outline}

The thesis follows a single arc, from the framework that generates
finite-domain proof obligations to the decision procedures that discharge them.
\Cref{ch:background} recalls the shared machinery: SMT solving over bitvectors,
bitblasting and SAT certificates, the Lean proof assistant, and the verified
\bvdecide{} bitblaster that the later procedures extend. \Cref{ch:lean-mlir}
builds the peephole-rewriting framework; by making the structural part of each
rewrite's proof free, it exposes the semantic obligations---goals over
bitvectors of arbitrary width---as the bottleneck that the remainder of the
thesis attacks. \Cref{ch:mono-width} takes up those goals for a single symbolic
width, and \cref{ch:multi-width} generalizes to many symbolic widths at once.
\Cref{ch:floating-point} carries the
same verified-bitblasting idea from bitvectors to floating-point arithmetic,
yielding the first verified floating-point bitblaster. \Cref{ch:future-work}
concludes and charts unpublished results and directions that extend this arc.
