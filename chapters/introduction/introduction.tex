\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:intro}
\epigraph{It is a laborious madness and an impoverishing one, the madness of composing vast books, setting out in five hundred pages an idea that can be perfectly related orally in five minutes.}{Jorge Luis Borges, Prologue to The Garden of Forking Paths}

\lettrine{T}he tradition of verified compilation~\citep{leroy2016compcert,kumar2014cakeml}
teaches us that verifying even a modest compiler end to end is the work of many person-years.
Consequently, I focus on enabling automated formal verification of the peephole rewriter~\citep{mckeeman1965peephole},
a large, critical, but modular component of a production compiler,
whose unit of work is a single, local transformation on a finite window of instructions.
Each transformation is small enough to state precisely and prove correct,
which has long made the rewriter the natural beachhead for verification.
For example, the Alive line of work on LLVM's instruction combiner~\citep{lopes2015provably,lopes2021alive2}
translates LLVM rewrites into SMT and discharges them automatically.
However, this makes the full SMT solver part of the trusted base,
and the Alive approach only checks a handful of widths for each rewrite,
both of which are gaps we aim to close in this thesis.

Toward this, I first formalized a core calculus for SSA-based compiler IRs~\citep{appel1998ssa,rastello2022ssa},
generic over the user's IR, and proved a generic peephole rewriter correct,
with case studies ranging from fully homomorphic encryption to LLVM's bitvector rewrites (\cref{ch:lean-mlir}).

Next, when building push-button automation to enable peephole rewrite verification at scale,
we wish to balance the competing concerns of \emph{performance} and \emph{trustworthiness}.
Subtle algorithms can be fast, but are hard to verify and change when the solver is updated.
On the other hand, simple algorithms are easy to verify, but can be far too slow to be useful.
I settled on bitblasting, an approach that enables both performance and trustworthiness,
since one can reduce a problem in a rich theory to propositional logic, and then dispatch it to a state-of-the-art SAT solver.
For example, Lean's fixed-width bitvector tactic, \bvdecide{} 
starts with a bitvector goal, performs bitblasting to produce an equivalent SAT problem,
dispatches it to a state-of-the-art SAT solver,
and has the resulting certificate rechecked by Lean's kernel.
This design  stands on the shoulders of the incredible progess in the last two decades
both in SAT solving, which now scales to hundreds of millions of variables~\citep{biere2024cadical,kissatSATComp2024},
as well as in UNSAT certificates, where the LRAT format~\citep{lrat} was developed to
enable SAT solvers to express all of their tricks, while still enabling fast verification of the result.
% The design of UNSAT cerificates is very interesting, since there is a tension between the solver's desire to express its reasoning in a compact way,
% and the verifier's desire to check the reasoning in a simple way.
Toward this, I helped implemet bitblasting in \bvdecide{}, which mirrors the state of the art SMT-solver Bitwuzla~\citep{niemetz2023bitwuzla}.
This provides push-button automation we needed to verify rewrites at a \emph{concrete} bitwidth (e.g. \texttt{i64}).
While this is a significant step forward, it is not enough.
An LLVM rewrite must be correct at \emph{every} register width: \texttt{i8}, \texttt{i32}, and even \texttt{i1729}.
The standard remedy against this, used for example by the Alive2 peephole rewrite verifier,
is to enumerate the widths and bitblast each one separately.


In this thesis, we show that such exhaustive enumeration is unnecessary.
For a fragment of bitvector theory, we show that predicates which hold at all widths reduce to reachability in a finite automaton,
which can be decided by $k$-induction, or by regular language emptiness (\cref{ch:mono-width}).
Next, by an encoding trick that writes widths as bitmasks,
any formula over $n$ symbolic widths reduces to an equisatisfiable formula at a single width (\cref{ch:multi-width}),
trading an exponential number of solver queries for a \emph{single} query and carving out a new sound and complete fragment of the multi-width theory.
% The reach of bitblasting, it turns out, extends all the way to the parametric bitvector theory.

Finally, we also wished to support floating-point reasoning, which is important for emerging applications in machine learning workloads.
Since machine learning is pushing for small, exotic formats such as E4M3 and E5M2 into production hardware~\citep{fp8fordl},
and these are precisely the formats on which existing solvers are least tested and most fragile,
it was important to us that our solver be verified at all widths, not just the common ones.
SymFPU, a state-of-the-art floating-point bitblaster, showed in a sequence of papers that bitblasting decisively
outcompetes the alternatives for floating-point SMT solving.
We follow this approach, and we mechanize the SMT-LIB \qffp{} theory,
and build verified bitblasting circuits for almost all of its operations.
Moreover, our bitblaster is formally verified in Lean,
thereby eliminating various trust issues around nonstandard floating-point formats and rounding modes.

In summary, this thesis builds a framework that decouples peephole rewrite verification from structural SSA manipulation,
and builds width-generic bitvector solvers that prove more problems than the unverified state of the art,
and builds the first formally verified floating-point bitblaster,
which offers a new level of assurance for mechanized floating-point reasoning.
I hope that this thesis provides new ideas for push-button verification for compiler optimization.

\section{Contributions}

\noindent First, we contribute \textbf{A verified framework for peephole rewriting in SSA-based IRs} (\cref{ch:lean-mlir}).
We formalize a core calculus for SSA-based IRs~\citep{appel1998ssa,rastello2022ssa},
generic over the user's IR and covering MLIR-style regions~\citep{lattner2021mlir}, mechanize it in
Lean with an MLIR-syntax frontend, and prove correct a generic peephole
rewriter as well as dead-code and common-subexpression elimination.
\publishedat{Published @ ITP~2024~\citep{leanmlir}}

\noindent Second, we develop \textbf{Certified decision procedures for width-independent bitvector predicates} (\cref{ch:mono-width}).
We mechanize key model-checking algorithms, such as $k$-induction, automata reachability, emptiness, and
minimization, and use them to build scalable, certified tactics for
bitvector predicates that hold at \emph{all} widths, plus a specialized
solver for mixed Boolean-arithmetic~\citep{liu2021mba,reichenwallner2022efficient}.
\publishedat{Published @ OOPSLA~2025~\citep{bhat2025certified}}

\noindent Third, we lift the mono-width restriction and obtain \textbf{Sound and complete solving for multi-width parametric bitvectors} (\cref{ch:multi-width}).
We prove that multi-width formulas over $n$ symbolic widths reduce, with linear blowup, to an equisatisfiable mono-width formula by encoding widths as bitmasks.
This translation collapses exponential enumeration into one fixed-width solver call and yields a new decidable fragment subsuming the prior state of the art.
\publishedat{Accepted @ OOPSLA~2026~\citep{bhat2026multiwidth}}

\noindent Finally, we construct the first \textbf{Mechanized floating-point bitblaster} (\cref{ch:floating-point}).
We mechanize the SMT-LIB \qffp{} theory~\citep{extrealsemantics} and build verified bitblasting circuits for its operations
following SymFPU~\citep{symfpu}, and we evaluate the resulting solver on SMT-COMP~\citep{smtcomp2024} and on new
small-format (E4M3/E5M2) benchmarks~\citep{fp8fordl}.
\publishedat{Submitted @ POPL 2027~\citep{bhat2027floatingpoint}}
