\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:intro}
\epigraph{Begin at the beginning, and go on till you come to the end: then
stop.}{The King, \emph{Alice's Adventures in Wonderland}}

\lettrine{C}{ompilers}, hardware designs, and cryptographic routines are
trusted by billions of users, yet the reasoning that justifies their correctness ultimately bottoms out in \emph{finite domains}:
machine integers of a fixed width, registers whose width is a design parameter,
and floating-point numbers laid out in IEEE-754 formats.
Two research communities have built impressive, but largely disjoint, tools for this kind
of reasoning. Automated SMT solvers such as Bitwuzla~\citep{niemetz2023bitwuzla}
and cvc5~\citep{barbosa2022cvc5} decide finite-domain theories with remarkable
efficiency, but are themselves large, highly optimized, and unverified
programs. Interactive theorem provers (ITPs) such as
Lean~\citep{demoura2021lean} offer foundational guarantees, checked by a small
trusted kernel, but have historically provided little of the push-button
automation that makes finite-domain reasoning practical at scale.

This thesis contributes to closing that gap. It began as an effort in verified compilation,
but I realized that a verified compiler must discharge \emph{thousands} of proof obligations over bitvectors
and floating-point values, and it is these proof obligations that resist mechanization at scale.
That discovery redirected the toward decision procedures that discharge such obligations.

From this work, we show that decision procedures for finite-domain theories can be implemented, proved correct,
and run inside a proof assistant at not too great a cost over unverified state of the art.
We support this claim with a sequence of mechanized artefacts in Lean.

\section{Verified Rewriting Demands Verified Automation}

Our starting point is compilation. A modern optimizing compiler is not one
monolithic transformation, but an accretion of \emph{thousands} of small, local
rewrites. For example LLVM's InstCombine peephole optimizer alone makes up roughly a tenth of all IR-transforming code in the project.
Each rewrite is individually simple, yet they coalesce into the whole,
and the compiler is correct only if \emph{every} one of them is.
Frameworks such as MLIR~\citep{lattner2020mlir} multiply this further,
encouraging many domain-specific IRs, each with its own family of peephole rewrites.

Verifying rewrites at this scale in an ITP simply does not work if human input in the loop is necessary.
Thus, we build two conceptual pieces, one to enable to  \emph{state and compose} rewrites over an arbitrary IR without re-proving the structural plumbing (e.g. SSA form).
Second, every rewrite discharges to a proof obligation over finite-domain values, for which we build verified decision procedures.
The framework we present in \cref{ch:lean-mlir} builds the framework,
making the \emph{structural} part of such proofs free.
The decision procedures we build in later chapters discharge the remaining \emph{semantic} obligations.

\section{How To Build Trustworthy Automation for Finite-Domain Theories}
\label{sec:intro:trust}

The tension at the heart of this thesis is one of \emph{trust}. On one side
stand state-of-the-art SMT solvers, such as Bitwuzla~\citep{niemetz2023bitwuzla},
cvc5~\citep{barbosa2022cvc5}, Z3~\citep{de2008z3},
which decide these theories with formidable speed and on which compiler writers and hardware verifiers rely daily.
Yet these solvers are large, aggressively optimized programs whose correctness rests on manual reasoning and testing.
On the other side stand interactive theorem provers such as
Lean~\citep{demoura2021lean}, Rocq, and Isabelle, whose every theorem is checked by a small trusted kernel,
but which have historically offered only weak, best-effort automation for these theories.
Isabelle's \emph{Sledgehammer}~\citep{blanchette2011automatic} delegates goals to external
provers, yet its bitvector reach is limited precisely because the underlying
solvers expose no fine-grained, checkable proof.
The practitioner is thus made to choose between speed and trust.

Several lines of work already try to bridge automated speed and kernel-checked trust.
\emph{Certifying} approaches run an external solver and check the certificate it emits inside the kernel.
Solvers such as SMTCoq~\citep{ekici2017smtcoq} reconstructs proofs from SMT solvers in Rocq,
and CoqQFBV~\citep{shi2021coqqfbv} checks a bitblaster's SAT certificate.
Moreover, systems such as F\textsuperscript{*}/HACL\textsuperscript{*}~\citep{swamy2023proof}
couple strong SMT automation to verified code.
In our case, rather than trust an external SMT solver and replay its output,

we reimplement and reprove the solver in the proof assistant, but we rely on SAT certificate checking,
which is a far better developed suite of techniques, both theoretically and practically, than SMT certificate checking.
We port the algorithms of the state of the art (e.g. SymFPU's floating-point circuits~\citep{symfpu}) into Lean and prove them correct,
so that each answer is trusted down to the kernel.

\section{Contributions}

Firstly, we contribute \textbf{A verified framework for peephole rewriting in SSA-based IRs} (\cref{ch:lean-mlir}).
We formalize a core calculus for SSA-based IRs,
generic over the user's IR and covering MLIR-style regions, mechanize it in
Lean with an MLIR-syntax frontend, and prove correct a generic peephole
rewriter as well as dead-code and common-subexpression elimination.
Published at ITP~2024~\citep{leanmlir}.

Secondly, we build \textbf{Certified decision procedures for width-independent bitvector predicates} (\cref{ch:mono-width}).
We mechanize key model-checking algorithms, such as $k$-induction, automata reachability, emptiness, and
minimization, and use them to build scalable, certified tactics for
bitvector predicates that hold at \emph{all} widths, plus a specialized
solver for mixed Boolean-arithmetic. Published at OOPSLA~2025~\citep{bhat2025certified}.

Thirdly, we lift the mono-width restriction, and build \textbf{Sound and complete solving for multi-width parametric bitvectors} (\cref{ch:multi-width}).
We prove that multi-width formulas over $n$ symbolic widths reduce, with linear blowup, to an equisatisfiable mono-width formula by encoding widths as bitmasks.
This translation collapses exponential enumeration into one fixed-width solver call and yields a new decidable fragment subsuming the prior state of the art.
Conditionally accepted at OOPSLA~2026~\citep{bhat2026multiwidth}.

Finally, we build the first \textbf{Mechanized floating-point bitblaster} (\cref{ch:floating-point}), where we mechanize the SMT-LIB \qffp{} theory,
build verified bitblasting circuits for its operations, and obtain the first
verified floating-point bitblaster, evaluated on SMT-COMP and on new
small-format (E4M3/E5M2) benchmarks. In submission at POPL 2027~\citep{bhat2027floatingpoint}.

\paragraph{Plan of this thesis.}
The \Cref{ch:background} explains the shared machinery of SAT solving, bitblasting, the Lean proof assistant,
and the verified \bvdecide{} bitblaster that the later procedures extend.
In \Cref{ch:lean-mlir}, we explain the peephole-rewriting framework.
Then, the \Cref{ch:mono-width} and \cref{ch:multi-width} chapters build solvers for mono-width and multi-width parametric bitvectors.
Finally, \Cref{ch:floating-point} explains how to perform verified-bitblasting for floating-point formulas,
yielding the first verified floating-point bitblaster. Finally, \Cref{ch:future-work}
concludes and charts unpublished results and directions.
