\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:intro}
\epigraph{You can't get to the moon by climbing successively taller trees}{Akin's Laws of Spacecraft Design (Law 31)}

The bitter lesson~\citep{sutton2019bitter} is the central observation in AI that search and learning
are the only two paradigms which keep paying off as we feed more compute into the algorithm.
The same appears to hold in automated reasoning, where search, exemplified by SAT solving~\citep{biere2024cadical,kissatSATComp2024}, now routinely
settles problems with hundreds of millions of variables.
Betting on this scaling has given us the bitblasting tradition of encoding rich theories into propositional logic~\citep{tseitin1983complexity,niemetz2023bitwuzla,barbosa2022cvc5},
which powers the state-of-the-art approaches to building SMT solvers.


This forces a question upon us: how do we, as practitioners of the art of verification, bring the paradigm of search into our own domain?
In my own area, that of compiler construction~\citep{aho1985compilers,lattner2004llvm}, and eventually, compiler verification~\citep{leroy2016compcert,kumar2014cakeml},
there are two broad answers.
The first is to reason about a single machine and a single program, both of which are finite, and are therefore in principle
encodable as a (potentially gigantic) SAT formula.
This is what translation validation and related approaches do, to great effect.
The second, and more satisfying, is to ask for correctness proofs that are quantified over
the programs and the machines they speak about.
This is a fundamentally unbounded problem, and so appears out of reach of search,
which, at least naively, demands a finite domain.

Mathematicians and logicians have pondered such questions too.
Compactness~\citep{boolos2002computability}, for instance, guarantees that an infinite set of first-order sentences
is satisfiable exactly when each of its finite subsets is, so that an unbounded question may be settled by finite means.
In this thesis, we follow the same design principle to reason about the infinite families of finite-domain problems that arise in compiler construction and compiler verification.

We focus on compiler verification. A compiler, in total, is extremely complicated, but each of its peephole rewrites~\citep{mckeeman1965peephole}
performs a single local transformation on a finite window, and is simple enough to serve as a bedrock for mechanization,
as witnessed by the Alive line of work on LLVM's instruction combiner~\citep{lopes2015provably,lopes2021alive2}.
A peephole rewrite parametrized by the size of the registers it acts on is once again an infinite-domain problem~\citep{niemetz2019towards,bit-precise-parametric-bv},
and we show that it too reduces to a finite-domain one, which can then be solved efficiently.
Finally, floating-point semantics~\citep{ieeefp} are defined over the infinite domain of the real numbers,
yet the rounding step~\citep{fphandbook} ensures that only a finite amount of information about a real number
survives into the result, so that finite-domain reasoning applies here as well.

Overall, the thrust of this thesis is to create, mechanize, and exploit reductions from the infinite to the finite,
and to apply these techniques to infinite-domain problems in compiler verification.
With finiteness comes tractability, with tractability comes automation, and with automation comes scale.
Our ambition is to carry compiler verification up to the volume of a production compiler such as LLVM,
where the peephole rewriter alone is larger than the entire vectorizer!

A second theme running throughout this thesis is that of mechanization.
That is, we do not merely devise new algorithms and implement new solvers:
we do so inside a proof assistant, Lean~\citep{demoura2021lean}, and prove our algorithms correct in its proof language.
Is this really worth the effort? Only time will tell, but I believe that mechanization will only grow in importance.
We live in interesting times (as of July 2026),
in which large language models~\citep{brown2020language,touvron2023llama} produce impressive volumes of code that cannot be trusted to get the details right.
Worse, such models regularly reward hack, fulfilling the letter but not the spirit
of the specification they are handed, which makes a machine-checked specification all the more valuable.
In this thesis, we show that mechanization is possible and practical across a wide range of the automation that large-scale compiler verification demands,
and we hope that our techniques enable future mechanization of even greater ambition.

\section{Contributions}

\noindent First, we contribute \textbf{A verified framework for peephole rewriting in SSA-based IRs} (\cref{ch:lean-mlir}).
We formalize a core calculus for SSA-based IRs~\citep{appel1998ssa,rastello2022ssa},
generic over the user's IR and covering MLIR-style regions~\citep{lattner2020mlir}, mechanize it in
Lean with an MLIR-syntax frontend, and prove correct a generic peephole
rewriter as well as dead-code and common-subexpression elimination.
\publishedat{Published @ ITP~2024~\citep{leanmlir}}

\noindent Second, we develop \textbf{Certified decision procedures for width-independent bitvector predicates} (\cref{ch:mono-width}).
We mechanize key model-checking algorithms, such as $k$-induction, automata reachability, emptiness, and
minimization, and use them to build scalable, certified tactics for
bitvector predicates that hold at \emph{all} widths, plus a specialized
solver for mixed Boolean-arithmetic~\citep{liu2021mba,reichenwallner2022efficient}.
\publishedat{Published @ OOPSLA~2025~\citep{bhat2025certified}}

\noindent Third, we lift the mono-width restriction and obtain \textbf{Sound and complete solving for multi-width parametric bitvectors} (\cref{ch:multi-width}).
We prove that multi-width formulas over $n$ symbolic widths reduce, with linear blowup, to an equisatisfiable mono-width formula by encoding widths as bitmasks.
This translation collapses exponential enumeration into one fixed-width solver call and yields a new decidable fragment subsuming the prior state of the art.
\publishedat{Accepted with minor revision @ OOPSLA~2026~\citep{bhat2026multiwidth}}

\noindent Finally, we construct the first \textbf{Mechanized floating-point bitblaster} (\cref{ch:floating-point}).
We mechanize the SMT-LIB \qffp{} theory~\citep{smtlibfpa} and build verified bitblasting circuits for its operations
following SymFPU~\citep{symfpu}, and we evaluate the resulting solver on SMT-COMP~\citep{smtcomp2024} and on new
small-format (E4M3/E5M2) benchmarks~\citep{fp8fordl}.
\publishedat{Submitted @ POPL 2027~\citep{bhat2027floatingpoint}}

\paragraph{Publications and Attribution.}
Each contribution above is based on the publication whose venue is named beside it,
and the corresponding chapter reuses text from that paper.
All of these works are collaborative; throughout the thesis, ``we'' refers to myself and my coauthors,
and my own contributions are stated at the beginning of each chapter in an attribution note.
We also draw on ``Interactive Bitvector Reasoning using Verified Bit-Blasting''~\citep{leanbv} (OOPSLA 2025),
the fixed-width bitvector bitblasting paper on which I am second author,
and reuse text from it in the exposition of \bvdecide{} in the background chapter (\cref{ch:background}).
That work is presented as background rather than as a contribution of this thesis.

\paragraph{Plan of This Thesis.}
We begin by surveying the shared machinery of SAT solving, bitblasting, the Lean proof assistant,
and the verified \bvdecide{} bitblaster that the later procedures extend (\cref{ch:background}).
We then present the peephole-rewriting framework (\cref{ch:lean-mlir}).
With this framework in hand, we build solvers for parametric bitvectors,
first for a single symbolic width (\cref{ch:mono-width}), and then for many symbolic widths at once (\cref{ch:multi-width}).
We next turn to floating-point formulas and show how to bitblast them in a verified fashion,
yielding the first verified floating-point bitblaster (\cref{ch:floating-point}).
We conclude by charting unpublished results and future directions (\cref{ch:future-work}).
